Week 3 · Working with models

Text Classification

Is this movie review happy? I asked a 22-million-parameter reader and a 124-million-parameter writer, four different ways, on 1,066 held-out reviews. The approach that failed completely is the one everyone tries first, and the diagnosis took one more measurement than I expected.

≈ 11 min read Last verified 2026-08-31 Working notes
Skip test

Answer these three and you can jump straight to the lab:

  1. Representation models and generation models process the same sentence differently. Which one lets every position see the whole sentence, and did that advantage show up in this week’s measurements?
  2. In the frozen recipe (embed every review once, then fit a logistic regression on the vectors), how many parameters actually train?
  3. Prompted for sentiment, GPT-2 small scores 0.502 and says “positive” for 1,064 of 1,066 reviews. The same forward pass’s two-token score margin ranks the reviews with AUC 0.797. What broke: the knowledge, or the interface?

#3 cost me the least compute and surprised me the most: the model was not confused, and proving that took a measurement I almost skipped.

Weeks 1 and 2 stayed inside the model: how text becomes vectors, and how twelve blocks edit them. This week the model gets a job. Sentiment classification is the smallest real task I know: one sentence in, one bit out, and a labeled test set to keep everyone honest.

The reading draws a line through the whole topic: representation models read, generation models write, and classification wants a reader. I have one of each on hand from the previous weeks, so I put the slogan to work. One caution before the numbers, because it changes what they can prove: this is a comparison of two specific checkpoints, not of two architectures. The models differ in size, training data, objective, and pipeline all at once, so nothing below isolates the direction of attention. What the measurements can do is test the practical advice the slogan encodes, and they complicate it in useful ways.

One question, 1,066 reviews

The data is Pang and Lee’s 2005 sentence-polarity corpus: 10,662 one-sentence movie-review snippets, each labeled by the polarity of the review it came from, in the 80/10/10 packaging the Hugging Face rotten_tomatoes dataset established. That gives 8,530 training sentences and 1,066 held-out test sentences, each split exactly half positive; there is also a 1,066-sentence validation slice that this week never touches, because nothing here is tuned: every knob below is a library default. One test example, labeled positive:

“lovingly photographed in the manner of a golden book sprung to life , stuart little 2 manages sweetness largely without stickiness .”

The contestants: all-MiniLM-L6-v2, a 22.7-million-parameter sentence encoder (the reader, and the model Week 1 used for retrieval), and GPT-2 small, the 124-million-parameter writer Weeks 1 and 2 dissected. Every number below is from executed code; the notebook reproduces all of it.

Show reasoning

Measured: the writer’s frozen features score 0.800 against the reader’s 0.774. Two honesty notes before crowning anyone. A paired McNemar test puts that 2.6-point gap at p = 0.08 (the two heads disagree on 238 reviews, 133 of them the writer’s way): suggestive, not settled, on 1,066 examples. And the comparison is between pipelines, not architectures; the preprocessing section below shows one pipeline flag moving the writer by 20 points. The reader’s real case is economics: within 2.6 points at 5.5× fewer parameters, embedding the corpus in 7 seconds against 24.

Frozen features and a 385-parameter head

The frozen recipe treats a language model the way Week 1 treated it: as a machine that turns text into vectors. Embed all 8,530 training reviews once, fit a logistic regression on the vectors, and the “training” of the classifier never touches the model at all. For the reader, pooling is what the model was built for. For the writer I mean-pooled the final hidden states over the real tokens: the same vectors Week 2 read with the logit lens. Measured:

The first surprise is the second row. The slogan says classification wants a reader, and my money was on MiniLM; instead the writer’s frozen features score 2.6 points higher. Honesty requires two qualifiers. A paired test (McNemar) puts that gap at p = 0.08 on 1,066 reviews: real enough to notice, not enough to settle. And the label-scarcity refuge I expected to hand the reader turned out to be an artifact: my first single-seed curve showed the reader leading at 50 labels, and rerunning every subset across ten seeds erased it. The means put the writer ahead at every budget, inside wide, overlapping bands at the small ones:

So where does the slogan stand? On raw frozen accuracy, behind. On economics, ahead: the reader stays within 2.6 points of a model 5.5× its size, embeds the corpus 3.5× faster, and its vectors are half the width, which halves the head, the storage, and later the vector-database bill. Per parameter and per second, the reader wins everywhere; per point of accuracy with labels to burn, it does not.

One more measurement belongs here, because it nearly fooled me. The reader’s pipeline L2-normalizes its vectors by design; my writer pipeline did not. Normalize the writer’s vectors the same way and its accuracy falls from 0.800 to 0.604: one preprocessing flag is worth twenty points, dwarfing the reader–writer gap itself. So “identical heads” never made the comparison fair; the vectors carry their pipelines with them. Preprocessing is part of the model, and slogans about architectures quietly assume it away.

The compact version of the frozen recipe fits in one screen; with the pinned environment from the lab it prints the first row’s accuracy at one more decimal place, 0.7739:

Python 
from datasets import load_dataset
from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.metrics import accuracy_score

ds = load_dataset("cornell-movie-review-data/rotten_tomatoes",
                  revision="aa13bc287fa6fcab6daf52f0dfb9994269ffea28")

encoder = SentenceTransformer("sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2",
                              revision="1110a24")
X_train = encoder.encode(list(ds["train"]["text"]))   # [8530, 384], frozen
X_test  = encoder.encode(list(ds["test"]["text"]))    # [1066, 384]

head = LogisticRegression(max_iter=2000, random_state=0)
head.fit(X_train, ds["train"]["label"])         # 385 trained parameters
print(round(accuracy_score(ds["test"]["label"], head.predict(X_test)), 4))

Thirty-one seconds of teaching

The frozen recipe’s ceiling is set by features that were never told about the task. The next rung trains the whole reader: transformers puts a fresh classification head on the encoder and all 22.7 million parameters update on the labeled reviews. Two epochs took 31 seconds on my laptop’s GPU and landed at 0.827, above both frozen rows, on a model less than a fifth the writer’s size. What fine-tuning actually did to the representation is a question this week only raises: Week 6 measures it properly, with regressions to catch what the updating breaks.

Last contestant: skip training entirely. Prompt GPT-2 small with the review followed, on a new line, by “The sentiment of this review is”, and pick whichever of “ positive” or “ negative” the model scores higher. What accuracy does the writer get by writing?

Show reasoning

0.502, and the shape of the failure is the lesson. The two-token readout picked “ positive” for 1,064 of the 1,066 reviews (a rephrased prompt made it all 1,066), so it scores essentially the base rate of the balanced test set. But the model is not blind: the margin between the two token scores, ranked across reviews, separates positive from negative with AUC 0.797. The knowledge is present; the decision threshold the raw comparison implies is broken. The failure is calibration, not comprehension.

The writer, writing

The fourth row is the one that matters most for how this decade actually uses these models. Prompting is classification by continuation: describe the task in text, and read the answer out of the next-token distribution that Weeks 1 and 2 measured. To be precise about the mechanics, since precision turns out to be the whole story: nothing is generated. For each review I compare the scores the model assigns to exactly two candidate tokens, “ positive” and “ negative”, after the prompt, and take the larger. The measured prompt places the review first, then “The sentiment of this review is” on a new line (the notebook has the literal template).

That readout scored 0.502, and picked “ positive” for 1,064 of 1,066 reviews; a rephrased prompt made it 1,066 of 1,066. On a balanced test set, always saying positive scores the base rate by construction. My first draft stopped there and concluded the model never engaged with the question. That conclusion was wrong, and one more measurement showed it: rank the reviews by the score margin between the two tokens and the ordering separates the classes with AUC 0.797, about 0.72 accuracy if the cut is placed at the median margin instead of at zero. The signal is in the margins; the zero point of the comparison is broken. Both token scores lean so hard toward “ positive” after this template that the sentiment signal, which is genuinely there, almost never crosses the line.

So the same 124 million parameters, asked the same question three ways: 0.800 through a trained head on its features, roughly 0.72-worth of signal through the miscalibrated prompt margin, 0.502 through the raw two-token comparison. The capability degrades as the interface gets cheaper. This is the cleanest small demonstration I have of why prompt calibration and instruction tuning exist: at this scale and vintage, base-model prompting needs its zero point repaired before it classifies anything (Zhao et al. measured and fixed exactly this failure class in 2021), and models only started answering instructions reliably once they were scaled far past this size or trained on instructions outright, which is Week 7’s territory.

What I am keeping from this week is not the slogan and not its refutation. Three habits earned their place: compare pipelines, not architectures, because a preprocessing flag moved this task eight times further than the encoder-versus-decoder gap; put error bars or a paired test next to any gap under a few points, because my 2.6-point “win” is a p = 0.08 suggestion and my low-data “crossover” was one seed’s fiction; and when an interface fails, measure what the model knows separately from what the interface lets it say. Week 15 turns the first two habits into a discipline; Week 4 picks up the third.

What I read this week

Hands-On Large Language Models (Alammar and Grootendorst), chapter 4, runs this same task on this same dataset with a wider cast: task-specific sentiment models, embedding models with and without labels, and generative models. Its representation-versus-generation frame is the slogan this essay stress-tested, and its figures of the frozen recipe are the ones to study if the pipeline above moved too fast.

Muennighoff et al., MTEB: Massive Text Embedding Benchmark (2022) is the answer to the question this week raises but does not settle: which encoder should the frozen recipe use? Fifty-eight datasets’ worth of the same measurement, including classification tasks exactly like this one. The residency meets it again when retrieval arrives in Week 5.

Run it yourself

The fine-tuning cell takes about ten minutes on a free Colab CPU and under a minute on a GPU runtime; everything else runs in seconds. Sources & further reading

  • Pang and Lee, Seeing stars: Exploiting class relationships for sentiment categorization with respect to rating scales (2005): the sentence-polarity corpus this week trains and tests on, from the era when sentiment analysis was the field’s hardest problem.
  • Reimers and Gurevych, Sentence-BERT (2019): the training recipe that turned encoders into the one-vector-per-text machines the frozen recipe depends on; MiniLM descends from it.
  • Sun et al., How to Fine-Tune BERT for Text Classification? (2019): the systematic ablation of the choices the fine-tuning row quietly made — learning rate, layers, epochs — and what each one costs.
  • Zhao et al., Calibrate Before Use: Improving Few-Shot Performance of Language Models (2021): the systematic study of the broken-zero-point failure this week measured, with a content-free-prompt fix.
  • Radford et al., Language Models are Unsupervised Multitask Learners (2019): the GPT-2 report and its zero-shot ambitions, which this week’s fourth row tests, at the smallest scale the report shipped, on two prompts it never promised to handle.
