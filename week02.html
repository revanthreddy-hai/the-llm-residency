Looking Inside a Transformer

Ask GPT-2 to finish “When Mary and John went to the store, John gave a drink to” and it picks “Mary” over “John” at better than seven-to-one odds. This week: the machinery between the lookup table and that guess (attention, feed-forward blocks, and the residual stream), measured in place.

Answer these three and you can jump straight to the lab:

  1. A GPT-2 block holds an attention layer and an MLP. Which of the two holds two-thirds of the block’s parameters?
  2. In attention, queries, keys, and values each play one role. Which pair sets the weights, and which one carries the information?
  3. Read with the logit lens, GPT-2’s running guess for the sentence above is still generic at layer 10 (“them”), and the right name is top-ranked only at layer 11 of 12. What does that suggest about how the answer is computed?

#3 is the centerpiece measurement. I expected the answer to build gradually, and instead got a cliff.

Week 1 ended on a fact I measured but did not explain: the vector for “bank” leaves the lookup table identical in every sentence and arrives at the top of the network carrying its context. Whatever did the carrying lives in twelve identical blocks stacked between the two measurements. This week I opened one.

The reading was the chapter this essay borrows its title from, and it describes the machinery well. But a diagram cannot tell you where in the stack a particular answer takes shape, and that turned out to be a question small enough to answer with code.

A sentence only context can finish

The test sentence comes from the interpretability literature, where it has a job title (indirect-object identification):

“When Mary and John went to the store, John gave a drink to ___”

Any reader completes it with “Mary”: John was just mentioned as the giver, so the receiver is the other name. No table of word frequencies gets you there, because both names are equally common; the only clue is which name appeared twice in this particular sentence. That makes it a clean probe for context-moving machinery. GPT-2, measured:

Prompt ends “…John gave a drink to”Prompt ends “…Mary gave a drink to”
Mary 0.446 · them 0.212 · the 0.071 · John 0.060 John 0.302 · them 0.239 · the 0.129

Swap which name repeats and the answer swaps with it. The model is not completing a pattern about Mary; it is tracking who has been mentioned once. Everything below is about how a stack of matrix multiplications does that, and every number comes from executed code; the notebook reproduces all of it.

Each of GPT-2’s twelve blocks contains an attention layer and a feed-forward MLP. Where do most of a block’s learned parameters sit?

Show reasoning

Counted from the loaded weights: attention holds 2,362,368 parameters per block, the MLP 4,722,432 — the MLP is 66.6% of the block. The MLP widens each 768-number vector to 3,072, applies a nonlinearity, and projects back down, and those two rectangular matrices are the largest single share of the model’s parameters: 45.5% across the twelve blocks. Attention decides where information moves; most of the capacity sits in what happens to it afterwards.

The residual stream is a running draft

The number to hold onto from the diagram is 768. Each position in the input carries a vector of 768 numbers, and that vector is the only thing a block hands to the next one. Attention reads it, computes something, and adds the result back in. The MLP does the same. Nothing is ever overwritten; each block reads the same stream, adds its edit, and passes it on. By the last block, the vector above “to” has been edited twenty-four times (twelve attention edits, twelve MLP edits), and the final guess is made from that one vector alone.

The two kinds of edit split the labor unevenly and in opposite directions. Attention is the only mechanism in the entire network where information crosses between positions: it holds 2,362,368 of each block’s parameters. The MLP, at 4,722,432 parameters two-thirds of the block, never looks sideways at all; it transforms each position’s vector alone. Whatever “knowing that John already gave the drink” means, the looking-up happens in attention and most of the capacity for making something of it sits in the MLPs.

Q asks, K matches, V delivers

Attention gets described with an intimidating vocabulary (queries, keys, values) for what is mechanically a soft lookup. Each position publishes three vectors, computed from its slice of the stream: a query (what am I looking for?), a key (what do I contain?), and a value (what will I hand over if selected?). A position scores its query against its own key and every earlier position’s key (later positions are masked out), the scores become weights that sum to one, and the weighted mix of values is what flows back into the stream. Queries and keys set the weights; values carry the information. GPT-2 runs twelve of these lookups per block in parallel (the heads), each with its own 64-dimensional slice of the 768.

What the heads actually do with that freedom is more habitual than the mechanism suggests. I took the attention weights from the final position (the “to” that must choose a name) for all 144 heads and looked at where each one spends its budget:

  • Some heads are wired to the recent past. Layer 5, head 12 puts 0.9999 of its weight on the immediately previous token (“drink”), and 14 of the 144 heads put their largest weight on the previous position. A previous-token lookup is useful everywhere, so the network keeps dedicated machinery for it.
  • Most heads spend most of their budget doing nothing. 66 of 144 heads put more than half their weight on the first token of the sentence (“When”, which carries no answer), and the average head sends 0.443 of its weight there. Layer 6, head 2 is the extreme at 0.9997. This is the “attention sink”: weights must sum to one, so a head with nothing to say parks its budget on a default position. A head that looks busy in a heatmap may be idling.
  • A few heads single out the answer. At the final position, the heads with the most weight on “Mary” are layer 11 head 8 (0.582), layer 12 head 11 (0.529), and layer 10 head 7 (0.492). Layer 11 head 8 gives John’s two mentions only 0.145 and 0.088: it has found the right name, not just a name. What a head does with what it finds is another matter, and it is the punchline below.

Sorting attention weights is the crude version of a known result, and the comparison taught me more than agreement would have. Wang et al. mapped this exact task in this exact model in 2022, with causal interventions rather than sorting. Two of my heads match their circuit: layer 10 head 7 and layer 10 head 10 (0.362, fifth on my list) are “name mover heads”, the heads that copy the answer into the final position. But my two strongest lookers, layer 11 head 8 and layer 12 head 11, are their negative name movers: heads that attend to Mary just as hard and write against her, pushing her score down. Attention weights say where a head looks, not which way it pushes. Sorting them puts copiers and suppressors in the same list, and only surgery of the kind Wang et al. did tells them apart.

The compact version of the three measurements fits in one screen:

Python 
import torch
from transformers import AutoTokenizer, AutoModelForCausalLM

tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("gpt2")
model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(
    "gpt2", output_attentions=True, output_hidden_states=True,
    attn_implementation="eager")
model.eval()

prompt = "When Mary and John went to the store, John gave a drink to"
enc = tok(prompt, return_tensors="pt")
with torch.no_grad():
    out = model(**enc)

# who does the model pick?
probs = torch.softmax(out.logits[0, -1], dim=-1)
top = torch.topk(probs, 3)
for pr, i in zip(top.values, top.indices):
    print(repr(tok.decode([i])), round(float(pr), 3))

# where does the last position look? (layer 11, head 8; 1-indexed)
att = out.attentions[10][0, 7, -1]        # [seq]
toks = [tok.decode([t]) for t in enc["input_ids"][0]]
for t, w in sorted(zip(toks, att.tolist()), key=lambda x: -x[1])[:3]:
    print(repr(t), round(w, 3))

# read the running draft after every block (the logit lens)
ln_f, W = model.transformer.ln_f, model.transformer.wte.weight
for L, h in enumerate(out.hidden_states):
    v = h[0, -1] if L == 12 else ln_f(h[0, -1])
    print(L, repr(tok.decode([int((v @ W.T).argmax())])))

The residual stream can be read at any depth: apply the final layernorm and the output head to the vector as it stands, and you get the model’s guess-so-far. At which point does “Mary” first become the top-ranked guess?

Show reasoning

Measured with the logit lens, “Mary” is ranked 43,494th of 50,257 at the embedding layer, still 24th after layer 9, 2nd after layer 10, and 1st after layer 11 — a cliff, not a climb. That is consistent with where the name-mover heads sit (layers 10–11): the answer is copied into the final position late, after the middle layers have worked out that a person is needed at all.

Reading the draft mid-flight

The residual stream being a running draft suggests an obvious trick, which the community calls the logit lens: take the vector at the final position as it stands after any block, apply the final layernorm and the output head to it, and see what the model would guess if the network ended there. It is a probe with known blind spots (early layers were never trained to be read this way), but the trajectory it draws is the story of this sentence:

After layerTop guesspRank of “Mary”
0 (embeddings)“destro”0.7443,494
5“drink”0.202,624
8“the”0.48316
9“them”0.8424
10“them”0.762
11“Mary”0.611
12 (output)“Mary”0.451

Three things in that table were not what I expected. First, the shape: the draft is grammar before it is fact. By layer 9 the model is already committed to “give a drink to someone”: “them” at 0.84. Only then does the specific name arrive. The answer does not accumulate; it arrives, between layers 10 and 11, exactly where the name-mover heads sit. Second, the last block lowers confidence, from 0.61 to 0.45, spreading probability back toward alternatives like “them” and “the”. On this sentence the final layer behaves less like an author and more like an editor hedging the draft, and Wang et al.’s map names a suspect: one of the Mary-suppressing heads above lives in that block. Third, layer 0 decodes to junk (“destro”), a reminder that the lens reads early layers in a language they were never trained to speak.

One bug from my own run belongs in the notes, because it rhymes with Week 1’s lesson about probes. The library returns the final layer’s vector already normalized, and my first lens applied the final layernorm a second time: layer 12 came out claiming “the” at 0.06, flatly contradicting the model’s real output next to it. Normalizing twice is a silent no-op nowhere and a wrong answer everywhere. The fix is one line, and the check that caught it is worth stating: the lens at layer 12 must reproduce the model’s actual output exactly: if it does not, the probe is broken, not the model.

That is the whole machine: a stream of 768-number drafts, edited twelve times by a lookup that moves information sideways and an MLP that works in place, then read out once. Week 1 established that meaning enters through context; this week located the machinery that moves it, and, for one sentence, caught it in the act at layer 11.

What I read this week

Hands-On Large Language Models (Alammar and Grootendorst), chapter 3, is where this week’s title comes from, and its diagrams of the transformer block are the best I have found: the residual stream, attention, and the feed-forward layer drawn as physical plumbing rather than equations. It also covers the decoding loop and the KV cache, which these notes defer to the inference weeks Week 17.

nostalgebraist, interpreting GPT: the logit lens (2020) is the source of the mid-flight readout used above, written as a working notebook with all of its own surprises left in. The observation that the stream holds a readable guess at every depth, mostly formed by the middle of the network for ordinary text, is the frame I tested here; this sentence turned out to be one of the interesting exceptions where the top of the stack does visible work.
