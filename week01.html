Week 1 · Working with models

Tokens, Context, and Embeddings

The word “bank” enters GPT-2 as the same number and the same vector whether it sits by a river or approves a merger. This week: how text becomes ids, where meaning enters, and what that looks like measured on four sentences.

≈ 11 min read Last verified 2026-07-26 Working notes
Skip test

Answer these three and you can jump straight to the lab:

  1. In GPT-2’s tokenizer, “bank” with a leading space and “bank” without one get different ids. Why?
  2. Two occurrences of the same token start from the same row of the same table. Where does the difference in meaning enter?
  3. Measured with cosine similarity, BERT’s final layer separates the river sense of “bank” from the finance sense, and GPT-2’s final layer does not. Name one reason.

#3 surprised me when I measured it. The middle of this essay is about what I found.

Week 0’s model read text one character at a time, with a vocabulary of 27: the lowercase alphabet plus a boundary marker. GPT-2’s tokenizer has 50,257 entries. This week is about what lives in that gap, and about what happens to a piece of text after it becomes numbers.

The reading was two books’ treatment of the same topic, and they use the word “embedding” for different objects. Rather than pick a definition, I picked a question small enough to answer with code.

One word, two meanings

Take four sentences. Two use “bank” to mean the edge of a river, two use it to mean a financial institution:

  • A. They fished from the muddy bank of the river.
  • B. She sat on the river bank all afternoon.
  • C. The investment bank approved the merger.
  • D. He deposited the check at the bank.

Any English reader resolves the two senses without noticing. The question is what a language model sees. Everything below is measured: the four sentences went through GPT-2, through bert-base-uncased, and through a sentence-embedding model, and every number in this essay comes from executed code. The notebook reproduces all of it.

Pause and predict · 1 of 2

Before reading on: when sentences A and D go into GPT-2, how does the word “bank” arrive?

Show reasoning

In all four sentences, “ bank” (with its leading space) is token id 3331. The tokenizer is a pure text-to-ids function: it never looks at meaning, so it cannot resolve senses. And id 3331 indexes one fixed row of the input embedding table, so both sentences start from the same vector. Whatever separates the river from the money has to happen later.

How text becomes ids

GPT-2’s tokenizer is a byte-level BPE, for byte-pair encoding. Building one is a counting exercise: start with 256 symbols, one per possible byte, then repeatedly find the most frequent adjacent pair in the training text and merge it into a new symbol, recording the merge. GPT-2’s vocabulary is exactly that arithmetic: 256 bytes, plus 50,000 recorded merges, plus one special end-of-text marker, is 50,257 entries. Tokenizing new text replays the recorded merges, in order, on each chunk of the input.

The result is stranger than the description suggests. Here is sentence A as GPT-2 receives it, with · marking spaces that belong to the token:

They ·f ished ·from ·the ·muddy ·bank ·of ·the ·river .

Three things worth noticing, all of which generalize:

  • Spaces belong to tokens. “ bank” with a leading space is id 3331; “bank” without one is id 17796. To the model these are two unrelated entries, which is why the same word can behave differently at the start of a line.
  • Frequency, not the dictionary, decides the splits. “muddy” survives as one piece while “fished” shatters into “ f” + “ished”, because the merge list was built from co-occurrence counts in the training text, not from a list of words.
  • The id itself is arbitrary. 3331 carries no information except “row 3331 of the table.” Nothing about the number encodes riverness, money, or even wordness.

The first vector: a row in a table

Inside the model, the first thing that happens to id 3331 is a lookup. GPT-2 holds an embedding matrix of 50,257 rows, one per vocabulary entry, 768 numbers each. Those numbers are ordinary learned parameters: they start random and get adjusted during training like every other weight. Id 3331 fetches row 3331, the same row in every sentence, every document, every time.

The measurement confirms how literal this is. I compared vectors with cosine similarity, which scores how nearly two vectors point in the same direction; 1.0 means the directions are identical. At the input layer, the vector for “bank” in sentence A and the vector for “bank” in sentence D score exactly 1.0: the same vector, because “bank” happens to sit at the same position in both sentences. Between A and B the score is 0.9978, and at this layer the input is only the token’s row plus a position vector, so position is the only thing that can differ.

So this is the first thing people mean by “embedding”: the input lookup table. It is one vector per vocabulary entry, no context anywhere in sight. If the story stopped here, both senses of “bank” would share one identical representation.

What twelve layers do to one word

The story does not stop there. GPT-2 and BERT each pass those input vectors through twelve transformer layers, and each layer lets a position mix in information from other positions: BERT looks at the whole sentence in both directions, GPT-2 only at the words that came before. By the top of the stack, the vector at the position of “bank” is no longer a fact about the token. It is a fact about the token in this sentence.

Pause and predict · 2 of 2

Both models compute context-dependent vectors. Measured by cosine similarity at the final layer, which one separates river-bank from money-bank more cleanly?

Show reasoning

BERT, decisively. At BERT’s final layer, same-sense pairs of “bank” average 0.741 cosine similarity and cross-sense pairs average 0.453: a clean gap. At GPT-2’s final layer the averages are 0.983 and 0.984: no gap at all. Part of the reason is architectural (BERT attends in both directions, and it was trained to reconstruct masked words, a task that rewards representing meaning at each position). Part of it is a known measurement problem with GPT-2’s final layer, described below.

Here is the centerpiece measurement. For each model I extracted the hidden vector at the position of “bank” in each of the four sentences and compared all six pairs. BERT behaves exactly as the textbooks promise. At its input layer the vectors are near-identical across sentences, because they are the same table row. By the final layer, the two river-banks agree with each other at 0.791, the two money-banks at 0.692, and every cross-sense pair has fallen to between 0.373 and 0.541. The model was never told these sentences involve two senses. The separation emerged from mixing in context.

The compact version of that measurement fits in one screen:

Python 
import torch
from transformers import AutoTokenizer, AutoModel

sents = {
    "A": "They fished from the muddy bank of the river.",
    "B": "She sat on the river bank all afternoon.",
    "C": "The investment bank approved the merger.",
    "D": "He deposited the check at the bank.",
}

tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("bert-base-uncased")
model = AutoModel.from_pretrained("bert-base-uncased",
                                  output_hidden_states=True)
model.eval()

vecs = {}
with torch.no_grad():
    for k, s in sents.items():
        enc = tok(s, return_tensors="pt")
        toks = tok.convert_ids_to_tokens(enc["input_ids"][0])
        i = toks.index("bank")   # one occurrence per sentence
        states = model(**enc).hidden_states
        # keep the input layer and the final layer
        vecs[k] = {L: states[L][0, i] for L in (0, 12)}

cos = torch.nn.functional.cosine_similarity
for a, b in ["AB", "CD", "AC", "AD", "BC", "BD"]:
    sims = [round(float(cos(vecs[a][L], vecs[b][L], dim=0)), 3)
            for L in (0, 12)]
    print(a + b, sims)

GPT-2 did not produce that picture. I expected the same separation, weaker perhaps, and instead got nothing: same-sense pairs at the final layer average 0.983 and cross-sense pairs 0.984. By this measurement, GPT-2’s top layer treats “muddy bank” and “investment bank” as near-identical. The middle of the network shows a small gap (0.851 against 0.802 at layer six), and even that disappears by the top.

Part of the explanation is the measuring stick, not the model. Ethayarajh studied exactly this in 2019: vectors in GPT-2’s upper layers crowd into a narrow cone, so much so that even unrelated words score nearly as high as related ones, and cosine similarity stops discriminating there. So before concluding anything about the representation, I asked GPT-2 the question it was trained to answer: which token comes next? After “The investment bank”, four of its five strongest guesses are “’s”, “has”, “said”, and “is”: a company about to do something. After “the muddy bank”, the two strongest are “of” and “and”: scenery in the middle of a description. The sense information survived all twelve layers. My cosine probe could not see it.

What I am keeping from this is the difference between a representation and a probe. The context was in there; the first tool I pointed at it was too blunt. I suspect this will not be the last time this journal confuses “my measurement shows nothing” with “there is nothing.”

The third representation: one vector per sentence

Everything so far assigns a vector to each token. Retrieval systems, semantic search, and most “embeddings APIs” do something different: they pool the token vectors into a single vector for the whole text, using models fine-tuned so that similar texts land near each other. This is the other thing people mean by “embedding”, and the collision between the two meanings is, as far as I can tell, the single biggest source of confusion in tutorials.

I ran the four sentences through all-MiniLM-L6-v2, a small sentence-embedding model that maps any text to 384 numbers. Then I embedded two search queries and ranked the sentences by cosine similarity, which is retrieval in miniature:

QueryRanking (cosine similarity)
“financial institution”C 0.344 · D 0.275 · B 0.076 · A 0.037
“the edge of a river”A 0.513 · B 0.361 · D 0.143 · C 0.094

“Financial institution” shares no word with either finance sentence, yet ranks both of them far above both river sentences. The river query has an easier job (sentence A contains “river”), but the margins are wide in both directions, and that gap is what vector databases are built on.

One caution from the same run: the raw pairwise similarities between the four sentences are much messier than the query rankings. “She sat on the river bank all afternoon” scores nearly as close to “He deposited the check at the bank” (0.324) as to its fellow river sentence (0.366), presumably because both are short narrative sentences about a person at a place. Sentence embeddings judge whole sentences, not the word you happen to care about. Retrieval works despite this; it is worth knowing why it sometimes does not.

That is the pipeline this week traced. The word “embedding” gets used for three different objects along it: the lookup row, the contextual vector, and the pooled text vector. When a tutorial or an API says “embedding”, the useful question is which of the three it means.

What I read this week

Hands-On Large Language Models (Alammar and Grootendorst), chapter 2, is the cleanest walkthrough of this pipeline I found, and it is where I took the three-representations frame from: the chapter walks token embeddings, contextualized embeddings, and text embeddings as distinct objects, with the clearest diagrams I have seen on the topic. If this essay moved too fast, that chapter is the slower, illustrated version of the same route.

Large Language Models: The Hard Parts (Souza and Regenstein) enters the topic from the opposite end. Its vector-embeddings material (pages 104–115 in my copy) assumes you already have a sentence-embedding model and asks the production questions: where to store the vectors, how to chunk documents, when to rerank results. There is no tokenization in it at all; the chapter simply starts where this essay stops. I expect to meet it again properly when the residency reaches retrieval systems in Week 5.
